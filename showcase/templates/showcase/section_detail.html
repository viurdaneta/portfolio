Principal component analysis

With the dataset cleaned, transformed, and explored during the EDA phase, the next step is to move from describing the data to uncovering deeper structure within it. While the earlier analysis highlighted relationships between variables and potential redundancies, it did not yet address whether different types of driver seasons naturally emerge from the data. To explore this, we turn to two complementary unsupervised learning techniques: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Clustering Analysis (CA). Principal Component Analysis helps reduce the dimensionality of the dataset by transforming the original performance metrics into a smaller set of orthogonal components that capture the majority of the variance. In practical terms, this allows us to summarize complex driver performance profiles—spanning consistency, reliability, and scoring output—into a few interpretable axes of variation. Beyond simplifying the dataset, PCA also helps reveal which combinations of metrics explain the most differences between driver seasons. Once the main dimensions of performance are identified, clustering methods can be applied to group driver seasons based on their statistical similarity. Instead of imposing predefined categories such as “top driver” or “midfield driver,” clustering allows these groupings to emerge directly from the data. By examining how seasons cluster together, we can begin to identify distinct performance archetypes across the grid. Together, PCA and clustering provide a structured way to move beyond individual statistics and toward a broader understanding of how driver seasons compare and relate to one another. The following sections explore how these techniques reveal meaningful performance tiers within modern Formula 1 seasons.

PCA Loadings heatmap

The first principal component (PC1) explains the largest portion of variance in the dataset, around 47%, and can be interpreted as capturing overall driver performance and consistency. This interpretation is supported by the strong loadings on variables directly related to race outcomes. In particular, avg_finish (0.97), team_avg_position (0.95), and logit_dnf (0.92) load heavily in the same direction, while logit_top10 (-0.95) and total_points (-0.92) load strongly in the opposite direction. Together, these variables describe a performance gradient: drivers with high PC1 values tend to be associated with poorer finishing positions and higher DNF probabilities, while those with low PC1 values correspond to drivers who accumulate more points and frequently finish in the top ten. As a result, PC1 effectively summarizes the overall competitive level of a driver across seasons.

The second principal component (PC2), accounting for approximately 15% of the variance, represents performance volatility and risk–reward dynamics. This component is dominated by finish_volatility (0.80) and years_active (0.51), along with a moderate negative loading on position_delta (-0.62). These relationships suggest that PC2 captures how variable or unpredictable a driver’s results are relative to expectations. Drivers with extreme PC2 values tend to either greatly outperform or underperform their starting positions and exhibit larger fluctuations in race outcomes, making this component useful for identifying drivers who demonstrate high-risk, high-reward racing patterns.

PC3, explaining roughly 10% of the variance, highlights specialization in certain conditions and contextual performance effects. The strongest loading on this component comes from dvt (0.75), accompanied by moderate contributions from years_active (0.35) and negative contributions from position_delta (-0.43) and high_alt_success_rate (-0.39). This pattern indicates that PC3 reflects performance variation tied to situational or environmental factors rather than overall skill level. Drivers who appear as extreme values along this axis may demonstrate strengths or weaknesses in particular race contexts, such as track conditions or specialized racing scenarios.

The fourth component (PC4), accounting for around 9% of the variance, appears to capture specialization related to environmental conditions and experience. This is primarily driven by the very strong loading of high_alt_success_rate (0.81), along with moderate contributions from dvt (0.40) and smaller effects from experience-related variables such as years_active (0.17). These relationships suggest that PC4 distinguishes drivers who perform differently in specific environments, particularly high-altitude circuits, highlighting how environmental adaptation contributes to driver performance variability.

Finally, PC5, which explains approximately 6% of the variance, captures effects related to driver longevity and atypical career patterns. The dominant loading on this component is years_active (0.67), accompanied by moderate contributions from position_delta (0.45). This suggests that PC5 primarily reflects experience and career trajectory differences among drivers. Drivers with extreme values on this component may represent veterans with long careers or individuals whose performance relative to starting position differs systematically over time. While this component explains less variance than the first few components, it helps capture subtle structural differences in driver careers that are not fully represented by overall performance or volatility.

Together, these first five principal components explain the majority of variance in the dataset, providing a compact representation of driver performance while retaining information about overall results, consistency, volatility, specialization, and experience-driven effects.

The scatter plot of drivers along PC1 and PC2 provides a visual summary of performance archetypes. Drivers located in the lower-left quadrant (negative PC1 and PC2), such as Max Verstappen and Lewis Hamilton, represent high-performing and consistent drivers. This is because their low DNF rates and high top-10 finish rates translate into negative PC1 values (since PC1 loads positively on DNF and negatively on top-10), while their stable results across seasons lead to negative PC2 values. In contrast, drivers in the lower-right quadrant, such as Nikita Mazepin or Kevin Magnussen (in weaker seasons), exhibit poor performance: they have high DNF rates, low top-10 rates, and inconsistent results, producing high PC1 and near-zero or slightly positive PC2 values. Intermediate regions contain drivers with mixed traits—for example, some may have moderate finishing consistency but occasional standout results, which positions them near the center of the plot. This pattern demonstrates that PC1 largely captures overall performance and reliability, while PC2 captures relative consistency and variance in results, making the scatter plot a useful precursor for clustering drivers into performance archetypes.

Clustering Analysis

The PCA visualization suggests that driver performance does not form clearly separated groups but instead lies along a continuous spectrum. In the PC1–PC2 space, observations are distributed smoothly across the plot rather than forming isolated clusters. This pattern indicates that differences in driver performance are gradual rather than categorical: drivers transition progressively from highly competitive seasons to weaker ones, rather than belonging to sharply defined types. This is consistent with the nature of Formula 1 performance, where outcomes are influenced by a combination of driver skill, team strength, reliability, and race variability. As a result, the underlying structure of the dataset appears closer to a performance gradient than to a set of naturally separated clusters. Despite this continuous structure, clustering remains useful as a tool for segmenting the performance spectrum into interpretable regions. Rather than discovering completely distinct groups, the objective of clustering in this project is to identify driver archetypes or performance tiers that summarize common patterns across driver-season observations. By grouping observations that share similar PCA characteristics, clustering allows the dataset to be organized into a small number of representative profiles—such as elite consistent performers, competitive midfield drivers, or high-variance backmarkers. This segmentation simplifies the interpretation of the multidimensional performance data and provides a structured way to analyze how drivers distribute across different performance archetypes throughout the seasons.

K-Means

The next step is to segment the spectrum into interpretable groups using K-means clustering. K-means allows us to organize this continuum into discrete performance archetypes, providing a framework for identifying patterns across driver seasons. To determine an appropriate number of clusters, we evaluated candidate values using the elbow method and silhouette scores, complemented by cluster quality metrics such as the Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz indices. These analyses ensure that the chosen clusters meaningfully summarize the data, balancing cohesion within clusters and separation between clusters. By partitioning the PCA-transformed dataset in this way, we can define representative groups of drivers—such as consistently dominant performers, competitive midfielders, or high-variance backmarkers—allowing for structured comparisons across seasons while retaining the nuanced insights revealed by the principal components.

Elbow Method, Silhouette Score, Davies–Bouldin Index, and Calinski–Harabasz Index

To decide how many clusters to use, I looked at silhouette scores, the Davies–Bouldin Index, and the Calinski–Harabasz Index. The silhouette scores slowly decrease as K increases (K=2: 0.344, K=3: 0.247, K=4: 0.230, K=5: 0.218). That’s actually pretty normal here because driver performance doesn’t fall into perfectly separate groups—it’s more of a continuous gradient from bad seasons to great seasons. Looking at the silhouette plots, the clusters also look a bit more balanced in size for K=4 compared to K=5, meaning the four clusters are distributed a little more evenly. That said, the difference in silhouette score between K=4 and K=5 is pretty small (0.230 vs 0.218), so adding another cluster doesn’t really hurt the overall clustering quality much. When we bring in the other metrics, things get a bit more interesting. The Davies–Bouldin score drops from 1.582 with K=4 to 1.428 with K=5, which is about a 10% improvement. Since lower values are better for this metric, that suggests the clusters are less similar to each other when we use five clusters. The Calinski–Harabasz score does go down slightly (86.94 → 84.45), but the change is small and doesn’t outweigh the improvement in Davies–Bouldin. From a Formula 1 perspective, this also makes sense. Driver seasons tend to fall into performance tiers—like dominant championship seasons, strong competitive seasons, solid midfield years, struggling seasons, and true backmarker performances. Using five clusters helps separate these types a bit more clearly, instead of lumping nearby performance levels together like the four-cluster solution might. So even though K=4 looks a bit nicer in the silhouette plots, the better separation and the more realistic performance tiers make K=5 the better choice overall.

Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)

I will apply a Gaussian Mixture Model to the driver-season data to explore underlying performance patterns. This method identifies probabilistic groupings of seasons based on the PCA-transformed features. I evaluated different numbers of clusters using the Bayesian Information Criterion to find the best fit. The resulting clusters are then compared to the K-Means results to interpret meaningful driver performance tiers.

from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
for k in range(1,10):
  gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=k, random_state=42).fit(pca_df)

The lowest BIC score showed up at K = 2, which basically means that from a purely statistical point of view the data could be explained by two broad distributions. In other words, the model mostly sees something like a split between strong seasons and weaker seasons. But when we look at the results through a Formula 1 lens, two clusters is way too simple to describe what’s actually going on. In reality, driver seasons tend to fall into several recognizable performance tiers: dominant championship-level seasons, very strong contender seasons, solid midfield consistency, struggling seasons, and true backmarker performances. The earlier K-Means Clustering analysis suggested that somewhere between four and five clusters captured these tiers best. Even if the Gaussian mixture model sees two broad statistical groups, splitting the gradient further makes the results much more interpretable in a racing context. With five clusters, the model can separate subtle differences—like distinguishing a driver who consistently finishes P6–P8 from someone stuck fighting for P12 every weekend. Because of that, the five-cluster solution ends up being more meaningful for understanding driver-season archetypes, even if the underlying data can technically be approximated by fewer probabilistic distributions.

Cluster interpretation

Cluster 1 – Elite / Championship-Level Seasons

Cluster 1 represents the strongest driver-seasons, where drivers accumulate very high point totals and consistently finish at the front. Max Verstappen (2023) is assigned 100% probability with 530 points, reflecting a dominant season. Lewis Hamilton (2019) also appears here with about 99.50% probability and 413 points, while Valtteri Bottas (2019) has about 99.96% probability with 326 points.

Other examples include Fernando Alonso (2023) with about 99.22% probability and George Russell (2022) with about 91.68% probability. These seasons strongly match elite performance profiles, with only small overlaps into nearby clusters.

Cluster 2 – Very Strong Seasons

Cluster 2 captures very strong seasons that are slightly below the most dominant group. Nico Rosberg (2014) appears with about 97.97% probability and 317 points, while Nico Rosberg (2015) shows about 99.35% probability with 322 points.

Additional examples include Sergio Pérez (2023) at about 99.99% and Lando Norris (2024) at about 99.81%. Some seasons show overlap, such as Charles Leclerc (2019), which has about 78.74% in Cluster 2 and about 20.55% in Cluster 0, indicating mixed characteristics.

Cluster 0 – Solid Midfield / Consistent Points Seasons

Cluster 0 represents midfield seasons where drivers score moderate points but are not consistently competing at the front. Nico Hülkenberg (2018) has about 97.98% probability with 69 points, while Jenson Button (2014) shows about 99.67% probability with 126 points.

Some seasons fall between clusters. Fernando Alonso (2016) has about 59.87% in Cluster 0 and about 40.02% in Cluster 3, while Sergio Pérez (2018) splits between Cluster 0 (about 55.36%) and Cluster 3 (about 44.64%).

Cluster 3 – Lower Midfield / Inconsistent Seasons

Cluster 3 includes lower-scoring or inconsistent seasons. Sebastian Vettel (2021) appears with about 88.89% probability and 43 points, while Kimi Räikkönen (2019) shows about 96.93% probability with similar scoring.

Some cases sit between clusters. Jenson Button (2017) splits almost evenly between Cluster 3 and Cluster 0, while Mick Schumacher (2022) shows overlap between Cluster 3 (about 62.52%) and Cluster 4 (about 34.23%).

Cluster 4 – Backmarker / Low-Scoring Seasons

Cluster 4 represents the lowest-performing seasons. Roberto Merhi (2015) has about 99.99% probability with 0 points, while Robert Kubica (2019) shows about 99.53% probability with 1 point.

Other examples include Logan Sargeant (2024) at about 98.77% and Nicholas Latifi (2022) at about 92.15%. Some overlap exists, such as Antonio Giovinazzi (2019), which has about 83.14% in Cluster 4 but still some probability in Cluster 0.

The Gaussian Mixture Model assigns probabilities rather than forcing each driver-season into a single category. Many seasons show strong membership in one cluster (often above 90–99%), while others are split between clusters. This allows the model to capture gradual differences in performance rather than rigid groupings.

Key Insights From the Analysis

Several clear patterns emerge from the clustering results:

  • Distribution of performance: Most driver seasons fall into midfield or lower-performing groups, while truly dominant seasons are relatively rare. This reflects the competitive structure of Formula 1, where only a small number of teams consistently operate at the front of the grid.
  • Consistency drives success: Drivers with high top-10 rates and strong average finishing positions accumulate points more reliably, while higher DNF rates push seasons into lower-performing clusters. Sustained race-to-race results matter more than occasional standout performances.
  • Strong cluster confidence: The Gaussian Mixture Model assigns most driver seasons to clusters with very high confidence, suggesting that the clusters represent real statistical groupings rather than arbitrary separations.
  • Stable performance structure: While the number of strong seasons varies slightly year to year, the overall structure remains stable— a small elite group, a larger midfield, and a set of struggling campaigns.

Conclusion

This project explored how unsupervised machine learning can be used to identify underlying performance patterns in Formula 1 driver seasons. By combining PCA for dimensionality reduction with Gaussian Mixture clustering, driver seasons were grouped based on their statistical performance profiles rather than predefined labels. The results reveal that driver performance naturally separates into distinct tiers, ranging from dominant seasons with high point totals and strong finishing consistency to seasons where drivers rarely score points. These groupings align closely with how fans and analysts intuitively describe the competitive structure of Formula 1. More broadly, the analysis demonstrates how clustering techniques can uncover meaningful structure in sports data and provide a data-driven way to describe different types of competitive performance across seasons.
